What weight should I start a lift at?

Start with a weight you could lift for about three more reps than you plan to do. Then let the first session correct it.

The short answer

For a new lift, pick a weight where your last planned rep still feels like you could do about 3 more. If you plan 10 reps, it should feel like a 13-rep weight. Starting a little light costs you one session. Starting too heavy can cost you good form, or a week off with a sore joint.

If you already know a related lift

Lifts that use the same movement are closely related. If you know your barbell bench press, you have a good idea of your incline press and your dumbbell press. Some typical relationships:

If you knowRelated liftRoughly
Flat barbell benchIncline barbell pressabout 80%
Flat barbell benchDumbbell bench press72–83% for both hands together, so about 36–41% per hand
Back squatFront squatabout 80%
Bench pressOverhead press55–75% (a loose coaching range, not a measured one)

These compare one-rep maxes, so turn your known lift into an estimated max first (see how to estimate your one-rep max), apply the ratio, then pick a weight that leaves about 3 reps in reserve.

A worked example

You benched 80 kg × 8 and felt you had 3–4 reps left. For 10-rep sets, GainPath proposes:

  • Incline barbell press: 57.5 kg
  • Dumbbell bench press: 25 kg per hand
  • Incline dumbbell press: 20 kg per hand
  • Overhead press: 40 kg (a rougher estimate, since it's a different movement)

Each is snapped to a weight that exists: a real dumbbell size, or a bar you can load with your plates.

If you have nothing to go on

Start with the empty bar or a light dumbbell, do a set of 10, and add weight in small jumps until the set feels like 3 reps were left. Write that number down. Next session starts there.

After the first session

Your first weight is a guess. What you actually did is better information. From the second session on, go by how the last set felt. See when to increase the weight.
