What does reps in reserve (RIR) mean?

Reps in reserve (RIR) is how many more good reps you could have done when you stopped the set. Stopping with 2 left is "2 RIR". Going until you can't do another rep is 0 RIR.

Why it's useful

"3 sets of 10" says nothing about effort. Ten reps can be a warm-up or an all-out grind. RIR adds the missing piece, and it's the easiest signal for deciding when to add weight.

How to judge it

  • Rate the last set of the exercise. It's the most tired one, so it's the most honest.
  • Count only reps with the same form. A rep where you have to twist, bounce or cut the range short doesn't count.
  • It's common to underestimate what's left, especially when a lift is new. If you're unsure between two bands, pick the one with more reps left.
  • Use rough bands, not exact numbers: 5+, 3–4, 1–2, or 0 (failure). That's precise enough to pick a next weight.

Where to aim

A common target for working sets is 1–3 reps in reserve: hard enough to drive progress, but short of grinding every set to failure. When a new lift is unfamiliar, start with more in reserve, around 3. It's safer, and it gives you room to learn the movement.

RIR and RPE

RPE (rate of perceived exertion) on a 10-point scale is the same idea counted the other way: RPE 8 ≈ 2 reps left, RPE 10 = nothing left.
